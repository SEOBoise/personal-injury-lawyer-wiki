    Personal Injury Lawyer

    The role, the legal concepts, and the evidence behind injury claims.

    Sources checked September 28, 202632 references3 data figures

    Personal injury lawyer

    At a glance
    Also called
    Personal injury attorney
    Legal field
    Tort law and related civil claims
    Focus
    Legal remedies for injury-related harm
    Key issues
    Liability, evidence, damages, insurance
    Resolution
    Settlement or litigation, depending on the matter
    Jurisdiction

    General legal information. A claim depends on its facts and the applicable law.

    A personal injury lawyer, also called a personal injury attorney, represents people seeking a legal remedy for injury-related harm. The field sits largely within tort law, which addresses civil wrongs. Personal injury can involve physical harm, psychological harm, or both, and claims may arise from negligence, intentional conduct, or other recognized grounds of liability. An injury alone does not establish that another person owes compensation.¹

    The central questions are connected: Who may be responsible? What evidence supports the claim? Which losses can be recovered? What will legal representation cost? A useful assessment brings the accident, the medical record, the applicable law, the available insurance, and the client's objectives together.

    The discussion below concerns the United States. A national overview can explain shared concepts, while filing deadlines, fault rules, available damages, and the right to practice law depend on the relevant jurisdiction. The statistics describe different populations and years; they are not a count of viable lawsuits or a forecast of an individual recovery.

    What a personal injury lawyer does

    Legal representation can involve investigating an incident, evaluating potential defendants, organizing evidence, assessing damages, dealing with insurance claims, negotiating a settlement, and preparing litigation. Those tasks answer different questions. A medical bill may document an expense without establishing fault; a photograph may show an unsafe condition without explaining how it caused a particular injury.

    An initial case assessment is therefore a process of connecting facts, rather than assigning a price to an accident label. A useful conversation identifies what is known, what remains uncertain, what information is needed, and which decisions have a deadline. It should also clarify the work the lawyer is agreeing to undertake and the responsibilities the client retains.

    The client, the attorney, and settlement authority

    The attorney advises and represents; the client makes important decisions about the objectives of the representation. The ABA's commentary on Model Rule 1.2 identifies whether to settle a civil matter as a decision for the client. It is a model of professional conduct, while the rules adopted in the relevant jurisdiction govern actual practice.²

    For a prospective client, that distinction suggests practical questions: Who will explain an offer? How will the risks be discussed? Who will handle routine communications? What happens if the client and lawyer disagree about the next step? Clear answers make the relationship easier to evaluate before documents are signed.

    U.S. injury statistics and their limits

    National data show the scale of injury, but each dataset measures something different. A visit is not necessarily a unique patient; an insurance claim is not a court case; a fatality is not a determination of negligence. The following observations retain their original years and populations.

    Emergency care and unintentional injury deaths

    CDC's FastStats page reports the following U.S. indicators. Its emergency-department figure comes from the 2022 National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey; mortality figures use 2024 vital statistics.³

    Indicator Observation Year and population
    Emergency-department visits for unintentional injuries 26.2 million 2022, U.S. visits
    Unintentional injury deaths 197,449 2024, U.S. deaths
    Unintentional injury death rate 58.1 per 100,000 population 2024, U.S. population
    Rank among causes of death Third 2024, U.S. deaths
    Unintentional fall deaths 48,308 2024, all ages

    These figures establish public-health context. A fall death can appear within the overall injury total; the rows must not be added together. The emergency-care observation is older than the mortality observations and does not describe 2024 visits.

    Motor vehicle crashes, pedestrians, and cyclists

    NHTSA's 2024 crash overview reports 6,180,241 estimated police-reported traffic crashes, 2,422,195 estimated injured people, and 39,254 traffic deaths. The fatality rate was 1.19 per 100 million vehicle miles traveled. Its detailed tables distinguish road users and crashes involving large trucks.⁴

    2024 traffic category Deaths Estimated people injured
    Motorcyclists 6,228 86,887
    Pedestrians 7,080 71,635
    Pedalcyclists 1,103 52,887
    All people in crashes involving large trucks 5,340 161,201

    The truck row includes people outside the truck and can overlap other rows. NHTSA defines large trucks here by a gross vehicle weight rating above 10,000 pounds, including commercial and noncommercial vehicles. The injury estimates and fatality counts come from different statistical systems; neither table determines fault in a specific collision.

    Traffic fatality trend: reported data and an early estimate

    NHTSA's April 2026 release projects 36,640 traffic deaths in 2025, a 6.7% decrease from the reported 2024 total. It projects a rate of 1.10 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles traveled. The trend below uses that release's consistent series: final files for 2019–2023, the annual report file for 2024, and a statistical projection for 2025.⁵

    Nonfatal workplace injuries and time away from work

    Private-industry employers reported 2.5 million nonfatal workplace injuries and illnesses in 2024, down 3.1% from 2023, according to BLS. The total recordable incidence rate was 2.3 cases per 100 full-time-equivalent workers. Separate case-characteristic figures cover the combined 2023–2024 period.⁶

    Workplace measure Reported result Scope
    Cases involving days away from work 1.8 million Two-year 2023–2024 private-industry total
    Median days away from work 8 days Those days-away cases, 2023–2024
    Cases involving only job transfer or restriction 1.1 million Two-year 2023–2024 private-industry total
    Median days of transfer or restriction 15 days Those transfer/restriction cases, 2023–2024

    The two-year totals should not be presented as one year's injuries. These employer-reported observations also should not be treated as a complete national count of workers' compensation claims or civil lawsuits.

    Fatal occupational injuries

    BLS recorded 5,070 fatal work injuries in 2024, compared with 5,283 in 2023. The 2024 rate was 3.3 deaths per 100,000 full-time-equivalent workers. Transportation incidents accounted for 1,937 deaths, or 38.2% of the total; falls, slips, and trips accounted for 844 deaths.⁷

    The fatal census and the nonfatal employer survey above have different scopes. A fatal work-related road crash may also be included in traffic statistics, so combining totals across agencies can double-count events. These measures help identify types of harm that require careful investigation; they do not specify an employer's civil liability.

    Consumer products and emergency-department injuries

    CPSC's 2024 injury estimates illustrate the frequency of injuries associated with household products and activities. Table F reports approximately 3,046,000 emergency-department-treated injuries involving stairs, ramps, landings, or floors, and 1,107,000 involving beds, mattresses, or pillows. Other selected categories appear in the chart.⁸

    An injury involving stairs can raise different questions from an injury involving a defective manufactured component. The statistical category identifies the setting or product association, while legal analysis asks what duty, defect, conduct, and causal connection can be established.

    Dog-related homeowners insurance claims

    The Insurance Information Institute and State Farm estimate 28,450 dog-bite and other dog-related injury claims in 2025, up from 22,658 in 2024. Estimated payments reached $1,862.1 million. The average cost per claim was $65,450, compared with $69,272 in 2024: claim frequency rose 25.6%, while the average cost fell 5.5%.⁹

    These are insurance-industry estimates, not a census of all dog bites. An average claim cost is neither a typical jury verdict nor a promised settlement for the next injured person. Differences in severity, coverage, responsibility, and the mix of claims affect the average.

    Hospital harm and traumatic brain injury

    A 2022 HHS Office of Inspector General report, studying hospital stays in October 2018, found patient harm among 25% of Medicare patients: 12% experienced adverse events and another 13% experienced temporary harm events. The study included preventable and nonpreventable events. It is historical patient-safety evidence, not a finding that one-quarter of patients had medical malpractice claims.¹⁰

    CDC's TBI overview provides two differently dated benchmarks: approximately 214,110 traumatic-brain-injury-related hospitalizations in 2020 and 68,663 TBI-related deaths in 2023. The hospitalization estimate omits injuries treated only in an emergency department or other outpatient setting, as well as untreated injuries. These are selected published benchmarks, not a single current-year total of brain injuries.¹¹

    Severe injury and legal responsibility are separate questions. Brain injury, spinal cord injury, burns, fractures, or long-term disability can make medical documentation and future-care planning important, but the injury's name does not identify who is legally responsible.

    Personal injury cases and accident types

    Case labels are a starting point for organizing an investigation. The same event may involve several issues—for example, a work-related vehicle collision can raise traffic, employment, insurance, and product questions. For an initial consultation, an incident description is more useful than trying to select a legal theory alone.

    Car, truck, motorcycle, pedestrian, and bicycle accidents

    A collision file can be organized around the road users, vehicles, location, sequence of events, reported injuries, and insurance information. Useful questions include whether photographs exist, whether witnesses can be identified, whether a police report was prepared, and whether a vehicle or recording may contain relevant evidence. A lawyer can then assess which information actually matters to responsibility and damages.

    For a truck crash, ask which vehicle, driver, employer, operator, maintenance provider, or other participant needs to be identified. For a pedestrian or bicycle collision, describe the crossing, signals, visibility, and movements rather than assuming that the road user's category resolves fault. These are investigation prompts, not conclusions about liability.

    Slip and fall, unsafe property, and dog-related injuries

    For a fall or other premises-related injury, a factual account should identify the condition, who controlled the location, what warnings were present, when the condition was noticed, and what documentation exists. Conditions can change quickly; the date and location of a photograph matter as much as the image itself.

    For a dog-related incident, useful intake details include the animal's owner or handler, the location, what occurred immediately beforehand, witnesses, injury documentation, and any incident report. Ownership, control, conduct, and the relevant state's rules should be evaluated before assuming a particular result. The claim statistics above do not answer those questions for an individual incident.

    Defective products and product liability

    Product liability can involve a manufacturing defect, an unsafe design, or inadequate instructions or warnings. Potential legal theories include negligence, strict liability, or breach of warranty, depending on the jurisdiction and facts. The relevant participants can extend along the manufacturing and distribution chain.¹²

    A useful case file identifies the product, model, serial or lot number, purchase information, condition, instructions, and how it was used. Ask counsel how the item should be preserved and whether an inspection is appropriate. A product's presence in an injury report or recall database does not by itself establish the elements of a lawsuit.

    Medical malpractice and professional negligence

    Medical negligence concerns the care provided, the applicable professional standard, a claimed departure from that standard, and the connection to harm. A professional's conduct is evaluated in the context of that profession rather than solely by an ordinary person's experience.¹³

    An organized intake can identify the providers, treatment dates, condition being treated, disputed event, subsequent care, and available records. Ask whether the lawyer handles that kind of medical case, whether expert review is needed, and what procedural requirements apply in the state. A poor medical outcome and a legally supportable malpractice case are not interchangeable descriptions.

    Workplace injuries and workers' compensation

    Workers' compensation is a statutory benefits system for work-related injury or illness. It can limit ordinary claims against an employer, and state law supplies much of the framework; some workers fall under specific federal systems. It should not be described as simply another name for a negligence lawsuit.¹⁴

    An injured worker can ask counsel to distinguish a benefits claim from any possible claim involving another participant, such as a separate contractor or product manufacturer. Whether such a claim exists, and how any recoveries interact, requires an analysis of the actual employment relationship and applicable law. Ask which professional will handle each part of the matter.

    Wrongful death and surviving family members

    Wrongful death is a civil claim arising from a death attributed to legally wrongful conduct. State statutes determine who may bring or benefit from the action and which damages are available. Financial support, funeral-related expenses, and other recognized losses may be relevant; eligibility cannot be inferred simply from being a relative.¹⁵

    An initial discussion should identify the deceased person, family relationships, the estate's representative if one exists, and the circumstances of the death. Ask how the lawyer distinguishes the family's claim from any claim belonging to the estate and what documents are necessary to establish authority to act.

    Negligence, liability, and evidence

    Duty, breach, causation, and harm

    Negligence generally concerns a failure to use legally required reasonable care. A clear explanation groups its elements into duty, breach, causation, and harm. Causation includes both the factual connection and the legal scope of responsibility.¹⁶

    Concept Question it addresses
    Duty of care What legal obligation applied to this person or organization?
    Breach How did the conduct fall short of that obligation?
    Actual and proximate cause How is the conduct connected to the injury, factually and legally?
    Harm and damages What injury and legally recognized loss resulted?

    Some claims use other theories. Strict liability, for example, can impose responsibility without requiring proof of negligent conduct, but the claimant must still establish the applicable legal elements. It is not automatic payment whenever someone is hurt.¹⁷

    An evidence file should answer a question

    The following is a practical organization aid. It does not declare that a document proves a case or that every item is required. Use it to discuss what counsel needs and to avoid confusing an expense record with evidence of how an incident happened.

    Material to organize Question to discuss with counsel
    Incident report, names, timeline Who was involved, and what accounts of the event exist?
    Photographs, video, original digital files What do the images show, when were they made, and is context missing?
    Medical records and treatment chronology What was documented, and which effects require clarification?
    Bills and insurance statements Which charges, payments, adjustments, and balances need explanation?
    Payroll, attendance, or business records What work or income change is supported by contemporaneous records?
    Correspondence and offers What has each participant said or requested, and on what date?
    Product, vehicle, or location information What physical evidence may need a professional preservation plan?

    Keep original files and a separate working summary. Distinguish your recollection from information supplied by someone else. If an important record is missing, list what it is and who may hold it. That makes a consultation more productive without trying to resolve evidentiary disputes in advance.

    Expert witnesses and specialized questions

    An expert witness uses specialized knowledge to assist with issues beyond ordinary experience. Expert work may involve reviewing records, examining a person or site, and forming an opinion. The admissibility and scope of that testimony are separate legal questions.¹⁸

    For example, ask whether medical, engineering, accident-reconstruction, vocational, or economic expertise would clarify a disputed issue. Also ask what the expert is being asked to evaluate, what information is needed, and how the expense will be handled. The goal is a defined evidentiary task, not a longer roster of professional titles.

    Compensation and personal injury damages

    Compensatory damages address legally recognized losses. They can include financial harm and nonfinancial harm such as pain and suffering. Punitive damages serve a different purpose: punishment of sufficiently serious misconduct under applicable law. They are not an ordinary extra percentage added to every injury claim.¹⁹

    Medical expenses, lost wages, and future effects

    A practical damages discussion separates categories rather than presenting one unexplained total. The table is a preparation aid; availability, proof, valuation, and any limits depend on the claim and jurisdiction.

    Category to discuss Information that may help organize the discussion
    Medical expenses Treatment dates, itemized bills, payment statements, and disputed balances
    Lost income Work dates missed, wage records, and the basis for any claimed income change
    Future medical or work effects Professional assessments and the assumptions behind projected needs
    Pain and suffering A specific account of the injury's effects on daily life, supported where possible
    Other claimed losses The legal basis, supporting records, and whether a category overlaps another

    Settlement value is not a universal formula

    A serious valuation discussion identifies the disputed facts, the relevant law, the documentation, the available coverage, and the uncertainty. A national average from a selected dataset cannot substitute for that analysis. It is useful to ask which assumptions change an estimate and how confident the lawyer is about each assumption.

    Distinguish an amount requested, an offer, a negotiated settlement, a court award, and the amount ultimately distributed. These numbers answer different questions. Before relying on a case example, identify its year, type of claim, procedural stage, and what the published figure includes.

    Medicare repayment and the net recovery

    A gross settlement is not always the amount the client receives. CMS explains that Medicare can make conditional payments for care another payer may owe, with repayment required when a related settlement, judgment, award, or other payment is made. Its Benefits Coordination & Recovery Center administers the recovery process.²⁰

    Ask for an itemized explanation of fees, case expenses, and any repayment or lien issues relevant to the matter. Where an amount is provisional or disputed, the accounting should make that clear. Different payment programs and contractual arrangements should be checked on their own terms.

    Tax treatment also depends on what a payment represents. IRS Publication 4345 distinguishes physical-injury or physical-sickness compensation from generally taxable items such as punitive damages, and explains the treatment of previously deducted medical expenses. A settlement should not be labeled entirely tax-free without examining its components and applicable exceptions.²¹

    Contingency fees and case costs

    What the written fee agreement should explain

    A contingency fee ties the lawyer's compensation to the outcome under the agreement. ABA Model Rule 1.5 describes a signed written agreement explaining the method of calculation, relevant percentages, expenses, and whether expenses are deducted before or after calculating the fee. It also addresses expenses owed regardless of outcome and a written closing statement. Actual state rules and the specific agreement govern.²²

    Ask for a plain explanation of three separate items: the lawyer's fee, the litigation expenses, and amounts that may be payable to others. A phrase such as “no fee unless we recover” should be read together with the written treatment of costs. Ask whether a percentage changes at filing, trial, or appeal, and what happens if the relationship ends.

    Why the calculation order matters: an illustration

    Consider a hypothetical $100,000 recovery, $10,000 in case expenses, and an assumed 30% fee. These inputs are chosen only to show arithmetic; they do not represent a market rate, expected recovery, or recommended agreement.

    Calculation Fee on gross recovery Fee after deducting expenses
    Gross recovery $100,000 $100,000
    Fee calculation 30% × $100,000 30% × $90,000
    Attorney fee $30,000 $27,000
    Case expenses $10,000 $10,000
    Remainder before other obligations $60,000 $63,000

    The $3,000 difference comes entirely from calculation order. Neither column includes taxes, medical repayments, liens, or any other obligation. A real settlement statement should use the actual agreement and explain each deduction rather than borrowing the example's assumptions.

    Insurance claims, settlement, and trial

    Liability insurance, UM/UIM, and PIP

    Auto coverage can involve bodily injury liability, property damage liability, uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage, and medical payments or personal injury protection (PIP). These cover different risks and beneficiaries; policy terms and state requirements matter. NAIC's consumer explanation is a useful starting point for distinguishing them.²³

    For a consultation, bring the policy declarations and relevant correspondence if available. Ask which coverage might be involved, who is evaluating it, and which deadlines or requests require attention. An insurer's involvement does not itself establish the insured person's liability or the amount payable.

    From investigation to a civil lawsuit

    An insurance claim requests a response under a policy; a lawsuit invokes a court process. As a federal illustration, the U.S. Courts describes a complaint and service on the defendant, followed by case preparation that can include discovery, depositions, motions, settlement efforts, and a jury or bench trial. State procedures have their own rules.²⁴

    Stage to discuss Questions for a case update
    Initial assessment What facts and legal issues need to be clarified?
    Investigation and documentation Which records are still missing, and who is obtaining them?
    Claim presentation What is being requested, on what basis, and from whom?
    Negotiation or mediation What issues are disputed, and what does each proposal resolve?
    Litigation, if needed What has the court scheduled, and what decisions require client input?
    Resolution and accounting What documents, deductions, and remaining obligations need explanation?

    This is an orientation checklist, not a compulsory sequence. Stages can overlap, and a matter can end before others begin. Ask for the next concrete milestone and the information that could change it, rather than relying on a promised national timetable.

    What historical trial statistics actually show

    The Bureau of Justice Statistics estimated that bench and jury trials accounted for 4% of tort dispositions in state courts in 2005. The report was published in 2009. It is a historical observation about a defined court population, not a contemporary percentage for all personal injury claims.²⁵

    Subtracting 4% from 100% does not establish that 96% settled: nontrial dispositions can have other explanations. It also excludes matters that never entered that court population. A sound decision about settlement should use the facts and risks of the actual case, not a universal “most cases” percentage borrowed from an old dataset.

    State laws, filing deadlines, and shared fault

    Statutes of limitations and related timing questions

    A statute of limitations can bar a claim after a specified period. The period varies by jurisdiction and type of claim, and its starting point may involve the injury, discovery, or when discovery should reasonably have occurred. A national page cannot identify an individual's last filing day from the accident date alone.²⁶

    Ask a qualified attorney to identify the controlling jurisdiction, claim type, starting date, and any separate notice or procedural requirement. If the matter involves a public entity, a child, delayed discovery, multiple states, or a professional-negligence claim, raise those facts expressly. These are verification prompts, not a list of automatic exceptions or extensions.

    Comparative and contributory negligence

    Under comparative negligence, a claimant's share of fault can reduce recovery. Some modified systems also restrict recovery when fault reaches a specified threshold. Contributory negligence can operate more restrictively. The controlling rule and its application must be checked in the relevant jurisdiction.²⁷

    Do not treat a percentage assigned in an insurer's letter as the final legal answer. Ask what evidence and rule support it, whether other participants are involved, and what facts would matter in challenging or accepting that assessment. This is particularly important when the incident occurred in a different state from where a person lives.

    How to choose a personal injury lawyer

    Verify licensing and the lawyer's actual role

    The ABA's licensing directory points to the agencies that can confirm whether an attorney is licensed and permitted to practice in a particular state. Verify the individual lawyer, not just a website's business name or a directory badge.²⁸

    A national-facing firm or referral website should explain who would handle the matter and in which jurisdiction. Ask whether the person taking the first call is a lawyer, whether the matter may be referred elsewhere, and when the identity of the responsible attorney will be confirmed. A broad advertising footprint does not answer those questions by itself.

    Compare relevant experience and credentials

    Practice concentration and formal specialty certification are different concepts. The ABA describes accredited specialty-certification programs and state-sponsored plans; the meaning of a certification claim depends on the actual credential and governing rules.²⁹

    Useful comparison questions focus on the work: Does the lawyer handle the relevant type of incident? What issues would they investigate first? Who would manage a disputed medical or technical question? How would the team explain case costs? A large advertised result can be relevant background, but it should come with enough context to understand the claim, the firm's role, and the procedural outcome.

    Communication, availability, and decision-making

    ABA Model Rule 1.4 commentary addresses keeping clients informed about material developments and communicating settlement offers so the client can make decisions. These are professional-model principles; the applicable jurisdiction's rules control.³⁰

    Before hiring, compare the proposed communication process in practical terms. Ask whom to contact, how updates are delivered, what response times are realistic, and how urgent documents are handled. Ask whether a written summary of the engagement is available and whether the fee explanation uses the same terms as the contract.

    Evaluation area A concrete question
    Case fit Which similar issues have you handled, and what makes this matter different?
    Responsibility Who is the attorney responsible for the file and who performs day-to-day work?
    Jurisdiction Where are the lawyers licensed, and is additional local counsel involved?
    Economics How are fees, case expenses, and third-party obligations handled?
    Communication How will I hear about offers, evidence gaps, and important deadlines?
    Scope What work is included, and what requires a separate agreement or specialist?

    Prepare for a personal injury consultation

    A useful intake summary

    A short, accurate chronology is easier to assess than an undated collection of messages. Start with the event date and location, the people or organizations involved, the injuries reported, and the treatment already obtained. Identify what you personally observed and what came from another source. Bring records already available; ask the office how to supply sensitive documents securely.

    • List the incident date, place, and any report or claim number.
    • Identify participants, witnesses, and their known contact information.
    • Note treatment dates and the providers involved without trying to diagnose yourself.
    • Organize bills, insurance correspondence, wage records, and photographs by date.
    • Record offers, forms, or requests received and any stated response dates.
    • List previous communications with lawyers or insurers about the same matter.
    • Write down the questions that matter most: responsibility, timing, costs, representation, and the next step.

    If a document is unavailable, state that clearly. A consultation should help identify the next useful information. You do not need to collect every checklist item before requesting an evaluation.

    Questions to ask before signing

    Ask what the lawyer has agreed to do and what remains outside the engagement. Request an explanation of costs using the actual written agreement. Ask how a decision about settlement would be communicated and documented, and how changes in the case would affect the plan. If a different firm may receive the inquiry, ask for that firm's identity and the reason for the referral.

    Treat clarity as part of the evaluation. The ability to explain an uncertain issue, a missing record, or a potential conflict plainly is more informative than a promise of a specific result before the evidence has been reviewed.

    Glossary of personal injury terms

    These short definitions summarize the concepts explained above. The applicable legal meaning depends on context and jurisdiction.

    Term Plain-language meaning
    Personal injury attorney A lawyer working on legal claims arising from injury-related harm
    Tort A civil wrong that can support a legal remedy
    Negligence A failure to meet the applicable duty of reasonable care
    Liability Legal responsibility for an act, omission, or resulting harm
    Causation The factual and legal connection between conduct and harm
    Damages The legally recognized monetary remedy sought or awarded
    Claim A request for a remedy or payment; it need not be a filed lawsuit
    Plaintiff / defendant The party bringing a civil lawsuit / the party sued
    Settlement An agreement resolving disputed claims on specified terms
    Contingency fee A fee arrangement tied to outcome under the agreement
    Discovery The formal exchange and development of information in litigation
    Statute of limitations A legal time limit that can bar a claim
