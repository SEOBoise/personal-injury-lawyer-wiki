    Personal injury lawyer

    From Personal Injury Lawyer Wiki, an independent U.S. legal reference

    Personal injury lawyer
    Also calledPersonal injury attorney
    FieldTort law and civil litigation
    RepresentsInjured claimants; sometimes defendants
    Core issuesLiability, causation, evidence and damages
    Typical feeContingency fee, subject to written terms and local rules
    ResolutionSettlement, dismissal or trial

    A personal injury lawyer is a lawyer who represents people seeking a civil remedy for physical or psychological harm. The practice falls mainly within tort law, which addresses wrongful conduct and the losses it causes. Common matters include traffic collisions, unsafe property conditions, defective products, medical negligence, workplace injuries and wrongful death.[1]

    An injury does not by itself establish a legal claim. A claimant generally must identify a valid theory of liability, connect the defendant's conduct to the harm, and prove recoverable damages. The governing rules—including filing deadlines and allocation of fault—vary by jurisdiction.

    Practice

    Personal injury lawyers investigate events, identify potentially responsible parties and insurance coverage, preserve evidence, evaluate damages, communicate with insurers, negotiate settlements and, when necessary, litigate civil cases. Before accepting a matter, a lawyer may assess jurisdiction, deadlines, available evidence, disputed fault, the extent of injury, collectability and the expected cost of the case.

    The client ordinarily decides whether to accept a settlement after receiving legal advice. A representation agreement defines the scope of work, allocation of authority, fee arrangement and responsibility for expenses.[2] A lawyer's evaluation is not a guarantee that a claim will succeed or that a particular amount will be recovered.

    Case types

    Personal injury practice covers several categories of civil claims. The required proof and available remedies differ by jurisdiction and theory.

    Claim elements and evidence

    Negligence commonly requires proof of a duty of care, breach, actual and proximate causation, and legally recognized harm.[7] Other claims may rely on intentional conduct, statutory duties, warranty or strict liability. Strict liability can remove the need to prove negligence, but it does not remove the need to establish the elements of the applicable claim.[8]

    Evidence may include photographs, recordings, physical objects, incident reports, medical records, bills, employment records, electronic data and sworn testimony. Expert witnesses may address specialized questions such as medical causation, engineering, vocational capacity or future economic loss.[9] Relevance, authenticity, admissibility and preservation can be as important as the volume of material collected.

    Fault can be shared. Under comparative-negligence systems, an injured person's recovery may be reduced by an assigned percentage of fault and may be barred above a jurisdiction-specific threshold. A smaller number of jurisdictions apply contributory-negligence rules that can bar recovery for any qualifying claimant fault.[10]

    Damages and compensation

    Compensatory damages are intended to address proven losses. Economic damages may include medical expenses, rehabilitation costs, lost income and reduced earning capacity. Noneconomic damages may address pain, disability, disfigurement or loss of enjoyment, subject to the law of the jurisdiction. Punitive damages serve a different purpose and are available only when the governing law and evidence permit them.[11]

    A gross settlement is not necessarily the claimant's net recovery. Attorney fees, case costs, medical liens, insurance reimbursement rights and other obligations may require resolution. Medicare may seek repayment of conditional payments after a settlement, judgment or award.[12] Federal tax treatment also depends on the nature of the payment and any applicable exceptions.[13]

    Fees and costs

    Many claimant-side personal injury lawyers use a contingency fee, under which the legal fee is calculated from a recovery. The percentage, the stage at which it may change, and whether expenses are deducted before or after the fee must be defined in the written agreement and comply with applicable professional rules.[14]

    Case costs are separate from attorney fees and may include filing charges, record fees, depositions, investigators and expert witnesses. A prospective client can ask who advances costs, who remains responsible if no recovery occurs, how liens are handled, and what final accounting will be provided. Defense lawyers are more commonly paid by an insurer or client under hourly or other arrangements.

    Insurance, settlement and litigation

    Insurance often affects the practical handling of a claim. Relevant coverage can include bodily-injury liability, medical-payments or personal-injury-protection benefits, uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage, homeowners insurance, commercial liability insurance or professional liability insurance. Coverage depends on policy language and state law.[15]

    A claim may proceed through investigation, written notice, exchange of records, evaluation and negotiation. If unresolved, litigation generally begins with a complaint and service of process. Discovery can include written questions, document requests, depositions and expert disclosures. Motions may narrow the dispute before a bench or jury trial.[16] Many claims resolve by settlement, but settlement is voluntary unless a party has previously granted another person settlement authority.

    A statute of limitations sets a legal deadline and can bar a claim. The applicable period may depend on the jurisdiction, defendant, theory of liability, age of the injured person, and rules governing accrual or discovery.[17] Administrative notice requirements or contractual deadlines may apply earlier.

    Qualification and professional duties

    In the United States, a personal injury lawyer must be licensed in the relevant jurisdiction or otherwise authorized to participate in a particular proceeding. Licensing status and public discipline can be checked through state regulatory agencies.[18] Certification as a specialist is distinct from simply concentrating a practice in injury matters, and not every state offers the same certifications.

    Professional-conduct rules govern competence, diligence, communication, confidentiality, conflicts and fees. A lawyer should explain material developments and settlement offers so the client can make informed decisions. Ethical requirements are adopted and enforced by jurisdictions; American Bar Association model rules are influential but are not themselves a single nationwide code.

    Terminology

    TortA civil wrong for which law may provide a remedy.
    LiabilityLegal responsibility for a claim or loss.
    CausationThe required connection between conduct and claimed harm.
    DamagesA monetary remedy for legally recognized loss.
    SettlementAn agreement resolving a dispute without a final trial judgment.
    DiscoveryThe formal exchange and development of information in litigation.
    Contingency feeA fee tied to a recovery under written terms and applicable law.
    Statute of limitationsA legal deadline that can bar a claim.
